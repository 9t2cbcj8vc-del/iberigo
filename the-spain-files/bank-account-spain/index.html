Opening a bank account in Spain: practical guide (2026)

August 2026 · 8 min read · Requirements vary by bank and customer profile

A Spanish bank account is not a legal requirement for every newcomer, but it can make everyday life much easier. Salary payments, rent, utilities, insurance, taxes and local direct debits are often simpler when you have an account that works smoothly in Spain.

The confusing part is that there is no single document checklist that every bank must use. Banks have their own onboarding and anti-money-laundering checks, so two branches can ask for different supporting documents even when your situation is similar.

Can a foreigner open a bank account in Spain?

Yes. Foreign nationals can open bank accounts in Spain, including people who are not yet resident. The exact account available to you depends on the bank, your residence status and the documents you can provide.

For an ordinary account, a bank can generally decide whether it wants to enter into the customer relationship, subject to consumer-protection and anti-discrimination rules. Banco de España also points out that people who have difficulty obtaining a normal account may, in qualifying circumstances, have the right to request a basic payment account.

If you open a non-resident account and later become resident, tell the bank. Your tax-residence and identification details need to stay up to date, and the bank may move you to a different product.

What documents should you take?

Banco de España says banks first need to identify you. In practice, a newcomer should prepare more than the minimum because the bank may also need to understand your residence situation and the source of the money entering the account.

A useful document pack is:

  • Passport or national identity card — valid and in good condition.
  • NIE if you have one. EU citizens may be asked for their passport/ID together with their NIE.
  • TIE if you are a non-EU resident and already have the card.
  • Proof of address — for example a padrón certificate, rental contract or another document the bank accepts.
  • Proof of income or activity — employment contract, payslips, pension evidence, tax return or self-employment documents where relevant.
  • Evidence of source of funds if you are transferring savings or a large amount into the account.
  • Proof of non-resident status if you are applying specifically for a non-resident account and the bank requests it.

Important: do not assume that a NIE by itself guarantees account opening. A bank can still ask for additional information to meet its legal customer-identification and anti-money-laundering obligations.

From real experience: opening at Sabadell

My experience at a local Banco Sabadell branch: I first opened the account as a non-resident. The branch asked me for my padrón certificate, NIE and employment contract. Later, when my status changed to resident, changing the account to a resident account was much simpler: I only had to show my Spanish residence document/card.

This is one real branch experience, not a universal Sabadell checklist. Another branch, bank or customer profile may involve different documents. It does show why arriving with more supporting paperwork than the minimum can save a second visit.

Step by step

1. Decide what you actually need the account for

If you need salary payments, local direct debits and everyday card use, compare ordinary current accounts. If you are still outside Spain, ask specifically whether the bank opens accounts for non-residents and whether the process can be started online.

2. Compare the full cost, not only the headline fee

Look at account maintenance, debit-card fees, cash withdrawals, international transfers and the conditions required to keep the account free. Some accounts waive fees only if you pay in a salary, maintain a minimum level of activity or use additional bank products.

3. Prepare a complete document pack

Take originals and, for a branch appointment, copies. If documents are from another country, ask the bank in advance whether it needs a translation or additional certification.

4. Ask for the pre-contract information

Before you sign, the bank must provide information about the account's main conditions, including charges and how the payment services work. Read the conditions instead of relying only on what was explained verbally in the branch.

5. Check the IBAN and account details before moving everything

If having a Spanish IBAN is important to you, confirm the IBAN assigned to the account. An IBAN from another EU Member State should generally work for transfers and direct debits covered by the relevant EU rules; refusing it solely because it is from another Member State can amount to IBAN discrimination. In real life, however, a Spanish IBAN can still reduce friction with some local systems.

6. Update the bank when your situation changes

If you receive your NIE or TIE later, become tax resident in Spain, change address or start a job, update the bank. Banks periodically refresh customer information and may ask again for evidence of income or source of funds.

What should you compare between banks?

  • Monthly or quarterly maintenance fees
  • Conditions for fee-free banking
  • Debit-card cost and replacement-card fees
  • ATM network and cash-withdrawal charges
  • App quality and online identification process
  • Whether service is available in a language you are comfortable using
  • International and non-euro transfer costs
  • Whether you need branch access for cash, paperwork or future mortgage discussions

Large Spanish retail banks include Santander, BBVA, CaixaBank, Sabadell and Bankinter. Mobile-first providers can also work well for everyday banking. The best choice depends more on your required services and fee conditions than on the logo.

Do you really need a Spanish IBAN?

Not always. Under EU payment rules, an employer, company or service provider should not reject a valid IBAN from another EU Member State solely because the account is located in another Member State when the payment is covered by the regulation. The European Commission calls this IBAN discrimination.

That said, newcomers sometimes prefer a Spanish IBAN because it can make local administration and direct-debit setup more straightforward. Treat that as a practical convenience, not as a universal legal requirement.

What if a normal bank refuses you?

A standard commercial account is not automatically guaranteed. But Spain's regulated basic payment account is an important fallback. Credit institutions that offer payment accounts must offer basic payment accounts to qualifying applicants, including people legally resident in the EU, asylum applicants and certain people without a residence permit whose expulsion is impossible.

A basic account can still be refused in specific cases — for example if you do not provide the information the bank needs for anti-money-laundering checks, or if you already hold a qualifying current account in Spain.

How safe is money in a Spanish bank?

Ordinary current and savings deposits at Spanish credit institutions are generally protected by the deposit-guarantee system up to €100,000 per depositor, per institution. EU banks operating in Spain may instead be covered by the equivalent deposit-guarantee scheme in their home Member State.

A practical newcomer strategy

  1. Keep your existing account open while you settle in Spain.
  2. Shortlist two or three Spanish banks based on fees, branch access and account-opening requirements.
  3. Bring passport/ID, NIE or TIE if available, address evidence and proof of income/source of funds.
  4. Do not buy insurance, investment products or other extras simply because you feel they are required — ask what is genuinely mandatory for the account and what is optional.
  5. Once the account works properly, move salary and direct debits gradually rather than all at once.
